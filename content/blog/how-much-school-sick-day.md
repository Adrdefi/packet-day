## The short answer: rest is the assignment

Here's what nobody tells you at the 7am attendance-line call: a sick day has one job, and it's not school. It's recovery. A kid fighting off a bug is doing important biological work, and that work doesn't leave much brainpower for long division.

Zero school on a sick day is a completely valid, completely fine choice. Full stop. If they're truly wiped — fever, stomach bug, the full-body blah — the answer is: none. Let them sleep. Learning will be there tomorrow.

But most sick days aren't that. Most sick days are the in-between kind: too sick for school, not sick enough to sleep. And that's where the guilt creeps in, because you've got a kid who's *awake* and you're wondering if awake means they should be doing something.

## The framework: three kinds of sick days

Stop thinking in hours. Think in tiers:

**🛋️ Tier 1 — Full rest.** Fever, vomiting, genuinely miserable. School: zero. This isn't a decision, it's medicine.

**📚 Tier 2 — Quiet day.** They're up, they're bored, they're on the couch. School: 1–2 hours of calm, low-energy stuff spread across the day. Reading, drawing, a puzzle, a printed packet. That's it. That's a full, successful sick-day school day.

**🌿 Tier 3 — Bounce-back.** They seem fine but school says 24-hours symptom-free. School: a light half-day. Two to three hours, easy pace, no new hard concepts. Think review and reading, not new material.

Notice what's missing: there's no tier where you replicate a full six-hour school day at the kitchen table. That tier doesn't exist, because it shouldn't.

## Why 1–2 hours is actually plenty

A regular school day is about six hours, but here's the open secret: only a fraction of that is real learning. The rest is transitions, lining up, waiting for 25 other kids, assemblies, and lunch. One-on-one (or one-on-couch) learning is wildly more efficient.

An hour of focused, calm work at home covers what takes two to three hours in a classroom. So your "barely did anything" sick day? It probably matched a full school day's worth of actual learning. You're not behind. You were never behind.

## What "counts" on a sick day

Forget the schedule. On a Tier 2 day, any mix of these counts as school:

- Reading anything — graphic novels count, audiobooks count, the back of the cereal box counts
- Drawing, coloring, or a simple art prompt
- A puzzle, card game, or building toy (yes, spatial reasoning is learning)
- Journaling — even "today I was sick and it was boring" is writing practice
- A printed learning packet done at their own pace

What doesn't count: anything that ends in tears. If the math page causes a meltdown, the math page was wrong for today. Put it away. The whole point of a sick day is low pressure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will my kid fall behind from missing school days?**
No. A handful of sick days across a school year has no measurable effect on learning — kids catch up within days, and one-on-one time at home is far more efficient than classroom hours. What actually causes "falling behind" is months of disengagement, not a week of rest.

**Is any school required on a sick day?**
There's no law that says a sick child must do schoolwork at home — an excused absence is excused. (Homeschool record-keeping rules vary by state, so log it as a rest day if your state counts days.) The only requirement is the one you're putting on yourself. Let it go.

**What's the best low-energy learning for a sick day?**
Reading (anything — graphic novels and audiobooks count), drawing prompts, puzzles, card games, journaling, and a printed packet they can do at their own pace. Independent activities are best, because you need rest too.

**Should I grade sick-day work?**
Please don't. The fastest way to turn a calm sick day into a battle is to attach stakes to it. If they did the thing, the thing is done. Save the red pen for a healthy day.

**How do I handle make-up work after a sick day?**
You mostly don't. For elementary kids, just move on — the curriculum spirals back to everything. For older kids, have them ask the teacher what's actually essential (it's usually less than you fear) and skip the rest. Never try to "double up" to catch up; it backfires every time.
