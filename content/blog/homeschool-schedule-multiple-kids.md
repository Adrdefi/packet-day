*By Natalie, homeschool mom and co-founder of Packet Day.*

If you only have one kid, you can skip this post. The rest of us need to talk.

Here was our morning for a long time: my 10-year-old needed me to explain fractions. My 8-year-old needed me to hear her read. They both needed me at the same time, and I needed a third arm and a clone. By 10 AM I was frazzled, they were bickering, and nobody had learned much of anything.

If you're teaching multiple kids at different levels, you already know the math doesn't work. You can't be in two places at once. And the standard advice, "just combine them," only goes so far when one kid is doing long division and the other is sounding out words.

Here's what actually fixed it for us: a homeschool schedule for multiple kids built on one simple rotation. Not a color-coded chart in fifteen-minute increments (I tried that; it lasted three days). Just a rhythm where one kid works with me while the other works alone, and the alone work is good enough that I don't get tapped on the shoulder every four minutes.

## Start together, then split

We open the day together. Twenty minutes, everyone at the table: a read-aloud, a poem, something we all enjoy. (If you want ideas, here's [our 60-second version of a morning basket](/blog/morning-work-basket-homeschool).) This is the glue. Nobody's behind, nobody's ahead, we're just together.

Then we split. This is the part that used to fall apart, because "go do your independent work" meant worksheets that bored them or confused them, and the shoulder taps started within minutes.

## Make the independent work actually independent

The whole rotation depends on one thing: the kid who's working alone needs work they can do alone. At their level. Interesting enough to keep going without you.

That's a tall order with off-the-shelf stuff. The workbook is either too easy (done in ten minutes, now what) or too hard (mom, mom, mom). So here's what I do now: each kid gets their own packet, 11 to 17 pages, generated around their level and their obsession. My son gets a space packet at his level. My daughter gets a baking packet at hers. Each has its own character, its own puzzles, its own reading. They don't need me to explain what's on the page, because the page was made for them. And it's all printed, so it's [screen-free](/screen-free) too.

While one kid works through their packet, I sit with the other one for the thing that actually needs me: the new math concept, the tricky reading, the writing assignment. Then we swap.

## Our homeschool schedule for multiple kids, step by step

Here's our actual rhythm. Steal what works:

1. **Together time (20 min):** read-aloud, poem, or a quick science demo. Everyone.
2. **Block A (30 to 40 min):** Kid one does packet work alone. Kid two does the need-me work with you.
3. **Movement break (10 min):** non-negotiable. Snack, stretch, chaos.
4. **Block B (30 to 40 min):** swap.
5. **Together close (15 min):** everyone shares one thing they learned or made.

That's about two hours, and it covers both kids deeply. The old way, with both kids needing me at the same time, we got less done in four.

## A few things I learned the hard way

The alone work has to be at the right level. Too hard and you're interrupted. Too easy and they're done in ten minutes and bouncing off the walls. This is why the generated packet matters so much for us: it's matched to each kid's level, so the alone block actually holds.

Don't try to teach the same lesson to both kids unless the ages are close. Combining works great for history read-alouds, science experiments, art, and morning time. It doesn't work for math and reading. Let those be separate, and stop feeling guilty about it.

Give each kid a checklist they can see. Kids who can check off their own boxes interrupt you less. It's almost magical.

And protect the movement break. Skipping it to "get more done" always backfires by block B.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a homeschool schedule for multiple kids look like?**
Start together, split for the core subjects, and come back together at the end. Our version: 20 minutes together, two 30 to 40 minute rotation blocks with a movement break between them, and a 15 minute close. About two hours total, and every kid gets focused time with you.

**How do you homeschool multiple kids at different grade levels?**
Combine what you can (read-alouds, science, art, history) and separate what you must (math, reading). Use a rotation: one kid does independent work while you teach the other, then swap. The independent work has to be matched to each kid's level, or the rotation collapses.

**Can you use the same curriculum for kids of different ages?**
For some subjects, yes. History, science, and art combine beautifully across ages. But math and reading need to be at each kid's own level. Forcing one level on two kids frustrates both of them.

**How long should a homeschool day be with multiple kids?**
Shorter than you think. With no transitions, no lining up, and no waiting on a class of 25, two to three focused hours at home covers what takes six at school. ([Here's our breakdown by grade.](/blog/how-many-hours-homeschool-day)) Our rotation runs about two hours, and both kids are done by lunch.

**What is independent work in homeschooling?**
Work your kid can finish without your help: reading, puzzles, practice problems, drawing, journaling. The key is that it matches their level and holds their interest. When each of my kids gets a packet built around their own obsession, the independent block runs itself.
