## First: lower the bar. Then lower it again. 💛

A sick day is not a school day, and trying to make it one will make you both miserable. Your kid's job today is to rest and get better. Your job is to keep things calm and not lose your mind.

If they do one or two hours of quiet activities across the whole day, that's a win. That's it. That's the bar. Everything below is built around that.

## The two modes of a sick day

Every sick day runs on energy, and it changes by the hour. I think of it in two modes:

**🛋️ Couch mode (low energy):** They're horizontal. Maybe a little whiny. This is reading, audiobooks, drawing, and anything that doesn't require sitting upright.

**🪑 Table mode (medium energy):** They're up, they're bored, and they're starting to bounce off the walls. This is the danger zone — the moment where the tablet comes out "just for a bit" and suddenly it's 2pm.

Your goal: have something ready for table mode *before* it hits. Because scrambling for ideas while a bored, sniffly kid asks "what can I dooooo" is how screens win.

## Quiet activities that actually work

These are all low-prep, low-energy, and don't require you to perform:

- **Audiobooks + coloring.** A chapter book read aloud (Libby app, free with your library card) while they color or draw. This one buys you a solid hour.
- **The blanket-fort reading nest.** Let them take every pillow in the house. A kid who won't touch a book at the table will read for 40 minutes in a fort. I don't make the rules.
- **Drawing prompts.** Not "draw something" (too open) — give them a mission: draw your dream bedroom, design a new animal, map our street from memory.
- **Card games and puzzles.** Uno, a 300-piece puzzle, solitaire. Quiet, independent, weirdly educational.
- **Journaling with a twist.** "Write a review of being sick, 1–5 stars, would you recommend?" Kids find this hilarious and they'll write a whole page.
- **Window science.** Bird watching with a tally chart. Cloud identification. Tracking the weather every hour. It's science, but it feels like spying.

All of these are great. But here's the honest truth: on a real sick day, *you* are also running on fumes, and assembling a day out of six different activities takes energy you don't have.

## A sample rhythm (steal this)

You don't need a schedule. But if your brain wants one, here's a loose rhythm that works:

- **Morning:** Couch mode. Audiobook + coloring while you get yourself together.
- **Late morning:** Table mode hits → hand over the packet. This covers you for 1–2 hours.
- **Lunch:** Eat together, no agenda.
- **Afternoon:** Free choice — fort reading, puzzle, drawing prompts, or yes, some screen time. It's a sick day.
- **End of day:** Have them tell you one thing from the packet. That's your "school." Done.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much school should my kid do on a sick day?**
None is required — rest is the job. But 1–2 hours of quiet, low-energy activities across the day is plenty and keeps the "I'm boooored" spiral away. Think reading, drawing, puzzles, or a printable packet, not a full school schedule.

**What are the best quiet activities for a sick day?**
Audiobooks with coloring, blanket-fort reading, drawing prompts with a specific mission, card games, simple puzzles, journaling, and printable activity packets. The best ones are independent — you need rest too.

**Is extra screen time okay on sick days?**
Yes. It's a sick day, not a parenting referendum. A little balance helps — some quiet off-screen time in the morning, screens in the afternoon — but don't beat yourself up about it.

**What if I have to work while my kid is home sick?**
Lean on independent activities: audiobooks, a printed packet they can do alone (answer keys included so there's no hovering), puzzles, and drawing prompts. Set them up in one spot with everything they need, and check in between your own tasks.

**When should I keep my kid home vs. send them to school?**
This isn't medical advice — always check your school's illness policy and your pediatrician's guidance. As a rule of thumb, most schools ask you to keep kids home with a fever, vomiting, or anything contagious, usually until they've been symptom-free for 24 hours.
