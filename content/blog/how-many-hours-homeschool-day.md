It's the question every new homeschooler asks, usually while staring at a public school schedule in horror: *they do six hours a day, so do I have to do six hours a day?*

No. You don't. Not even close. Here's the real answer, by grade, and why homeschool hours work completely differently than school hours.

## The short answer

- **K-2:** 1 to 2 hours
- **3-5:** 2 to 3 hours
- **6-8:** 3 to 4 hours

Read that again if you need to. These aren't "minimums for struggling families." These are the normal, healthy, *recommended* ranges that experienced homeschoolers actually use. If you're doing more than this and everyone's miserable, the hours are the problem, not your kid.

## Why home needs fewer hours than school

A six-hour school day contains roughly two to three hours of actual learning. The rest is transition time: lining up, settling down, waiting for 25 kids to finish, assemblies, lunch, recess, classroom management. None of that exists at your kitchen table.

One-on-one instruction is absurdly efficient. A math lesson that takes 45 minutes in a classroom, with explanations repeated three ways for three different groups, takes 15 minutes with one kid who can just ask. Reading practice doesn't wait for a turn. There's no "while I help table three."

So when someone says "but school does six hours," the honest reply is: school does six hours *of school*. You only need the learning part.

## By grade, in detail

**Kindergarten to 2nd grade: 1 to 2 hours 📝**
At this age, attention spans are short and that's developmentally correct, not a problem. The non-negotiables: learning to read (phonics + read-alouds), basic math (counting, addition, subtraction), and lots of play, which is genuinely how little brains learn. An hour of focused work plus read-alouds and educational play is a full, rich day. If your kindergartener is "done" in 45 minutes, they're done. Let them go play.

**3rd to 5th grade: 2 to 3 hours 📚**
The reading-to-learn shift happens here, and the workload grows, but so does stamina. Aim for: math (30 to 45 min), reading/writing (45 to 60 min), and one rotating subject (science, history, art). Two and a half focused hours at home covers what takes all day at school. Afternoons are for projects, outside time, and being a kid.

**6th to 8th grade: 3 to 4 hours 🧠**
Middle schoolers can handle more, and the content gets meatier: pre-algebra, essay writing, real science. Three to four hours of focused work is plenty, especially if some of it is independent. This is also the age to start handing them the plan and letting them manage their own time. That's a life skill disguised as school.

## What the hours actually look like

Forget the bell schedule. A homeschool day is shaped like this:

- **Morning block:** the core. Math and language arts, back to back, while brains are fresh. This is 60 to 90% of the "hours" above.
- **Midday:** the rotating subject. Science one day, history the next, art when the mood strikes. Or nothing. Nothing is a valid choice some days.
- **Afternoon:** real life. Reading for fun, projects, outside, co-op, hobbies, chores (life skills count), being gloriously bored.

The families who thrive aren't the ones doing the most hours. They're the ones protecting the morning block and releasing the rest.

## When to do more (and when to do less)

**Do more when:** your kid is on a tear: obsessed with a project, begging for more math, deep in a book series. Follow the energy. Those are the golden days.

**Do less when:** someone's sick, it's a Bad Day, you're burned out, life is lifing. Shrink to the minimum viable day (one subject, one hour) or take the day entirely. The yearly math still works: 180 days of *average* effort beats 100 perfect days and 80 miserable ones.

**Ignore:** anyone who tells you a specific number is required. Some states have day-count rules for records, but "hours per day" mandates are vanishingly rare, and even where they exist, they count the whole rich day, not desk time. (Check your own state's rules to be sure.)

## The real metric

Hours are easy to count, which is why we obsess over them. But the real metric is simpler: *is my kid learning and are we both mostly okay?*

If yes, your hours are right, whatever they are. If no, the answer is almost never "add more hours." It's usually "change something": the material, the rhythm, the pressure, the scenery.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many hours a day should I homeschool?**
Grades K to 2: 1 to 2 hours. Grades 3 to 5: 2 to 3 hours. Grades 6 to 8: 3 to 4 hours. These are the normal, widely used ranges. One-on-one learning is far more efficient than classroom time, so fewer hours cover the same ground.

**Do homeschoolers have to do 6 hours a day like public school?**
No. A six-hour school day includes transitions, lining up, assemblies, and classroom management. Only 2 to 3 hours is actual instruction. At home, you only need the instruction part.

**What does a typical homeschool day look like?**
A focused morning block (math + language arts), a rotating midday subject (science, history, art, or nothing), and afternoons for reading, projects, outside time, and real life. Protect the morning; release the rest.

**Is 2 hours of homeschool enough?**
For grades K to 5, absolutely. It's the standard recommendation. Two focused hours at home routinely cover what takes a full school day in a classroom. Judge by progress and wellbeing, not the clock.

**Do I need to log homeschool hours?**
It depends on your state. Most require only attendance days or nothing at all, and "hours" mandates are rare. Check your state's current homeschool law rather than guessing. When in doubt, a simple daily log of what you covered satisfies nearly every requirement.
