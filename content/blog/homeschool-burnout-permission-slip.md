You know the feeling. It's February — or October, or a random Tuesday in April — and the thought of opening the curriculum makes you want to cry. The kids can feel it. You're snapping over spelling. Everyone's miserable. And the voice in your head is saying: *maybe I'm just not cut out for this.*

You are. You're just burned out. And burnout isn't a character flaw — it's data. It means the system is asking more than you have, and something needs to change.

This is your permission slip. Let's talk about what's actually going on, and the small shifts that pull you out.

## How you know it's burnout (not just a bad week)

A bad week ends. Burnout doesn't — it just sits there, week after week. Signs:

- **Dread.** Not tiredness — dread. The physical flinch when you think about starting school.
- **The snap.** You're correcting handwriting like it's a personal insult. The reaction is bigger than the trigger.
- **Fantasy school-shopping.** You've priced out three private schools this month. (It's never about the schools.)
- **Numb planning.** You're going through the motions, but you've stopped believing in the day.
- **Guilt on top of exhaustion.** You're too tired to do it well and too guilty to stop. That's the burnout sandwich, and it's the worst meal there is.

If you nodded at three or more: it's burnout, friend. Not failure. Burnout.

## The permission slip (official, laminated, everything)

I, Natalie, fellow homeschool mom, hereby grant you permission to:

- **Do less school for a while.** Not forever. A week, two weeks. The curriculum will wait. It is extremely patient.
- **Outsource without guilt.** A tutor, a co-op class, an online program, a grandparent who likes fractions — using help isn't quitting.
- **Declare a reset week.** No new material. Reading, documentaries, nature walks, board games, packets. Call it "review week" in your records and mean it.
- **Rest like it's your job.** Because right now, recovery *is* the job. A burned-out teacher teaches burnout.
- **Change the scenery.** School at the park. School at the library. School in pajamas on the floor. Novelty is a reset button.

Print this out if you need to. Stick it on the fridge. It's real because I said so, and I'm very official.

## What actually helps (not the platitude version)

You've heard "take time for yourself!" before, usually from someone with no idea what your Tuesdays look like. Here's what actually moves the needle:

**Shrink the day.** Pick the one subject that matters most right now (usually math or reading) and do just that, plus one fun thing. A 90-minute day for two weeks beats a 5-hour day that makes you cry. When energy returns, add back slowly.

**Automate one decision.** Burnout is often decision fatigue wearing a costume. Every "what should we do today" costs you. So remove one: generate a packet each morning — grade level, current obsession, 60 seconds, done. 12–18 pages, character woven through every subject, answer keys included. The day plans itself. That's one giant decision you never make again.

**Get one win on the calendar.** Burnout thrives in endlessness. Put something concrete ahead: a field trip, a co-op day, a friend visit, even just "pajama school Friday." Something to look at besides the curriculum.

**Tell someone.** Your spouse, your homeschool friend, your group chat. Say "I'm burned out" out loud. It loses half its power the second it's spoken, and you'll discover every single one of them has been there.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the signs of homeschool burnout?**
Dread at the thought of starting school, snapping over small things, fantasizing about other school options, going through the motions without belief, and guilt layered over exhaustion. A bad week ends; burnout lingers for weeks.

**How do I recover from homeschool burnout?**
Shrink the school day to the essentials for 1–2 weeks, automate decisions (like daily plans), get one fun thing on the calendar, change the scenery, and tell someone you're struggling. Recovery needs rest *and* reduced load — not just a weekend off.

**Should I take a break from homeschooling when burned out?**
A reset week (or two) of light, fun learning — reading, documentaries, games, generated packets — is often exactly what's needed. It isn't quitting; it's maintenance. Log it as review week and release the guilt.

**How do I prevent homeschool burnout?**
Build sustainable systems before you need them: automate daily decisions, keep one "easy button" activity in reserve, schedule regular lighter weeks, maintain a support circle, and protect your own rest like it's curriculum — because it is.

**Is it normal to want to quit homeschooling?**
Completely. Nearly every long-term homeschooler has priced out alternatives during a hard season. The fantasy is usually about escaping the overwhelm, not about the schooling itself. Fix the overwhelm first; the fantasy usually fades.
